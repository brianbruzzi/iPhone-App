# Creatably's Longform Ad Formula

Analysis based on auto-generated transcripts from 85 of Creatably's longform (>120s)
YouTube videos (5 of 90 qualifying videos had no captions available and were skipped).

## The shape of every ad (3 acts, ~2–4.5 min)

1. **Cold open (~20%)** — starts *inside* a scene, never with the product. No logo, no
   "are you tired of X." It drops you into a genre bit already in motion (a heist, a
   police stop, a courtroom, a fairy tale, a game show, a haunted house tour).
2. **Explainer via double-act (~55–60%)** — a skeptic and an expert bounce questions and
   answers back and forth. Every feature/spec gets smuggled in as an answer to a dumb or
   defensive question, not as a listed bullet.
3. **Proof + exit (~20%)** — a quick burst of "real" customer quotes, a price reframe,
   then a CTA stated 2–3 times, closed out with a callback joke to the cold open.

## The core devices, in order of how often they show up

**1. Genre skin as a Trojan horse.**
The ad borrows a whole recognizable format so it doesn't feel like an ad while it's
teaching you the pitch: spy movie (Bacon), Lord of the Rings (Luminous), heist film
(Kizik), superhero team (21 Again "Low T Force"), game show (Lab Grown), fairy tale
(iSleep's Snow White, Sheets & Giggles' demon-under-the-bed), medieval court (US Virtual
Notary), cult documentary (Cleancult), sports broadcast (Tough Apparel), true-crime doc
(Homie: The Hunting). The category never dictates the genre — a mattress gets French
Revolution, a credit union gets a supervillain monologue. The genre is picked for comedic
mileage, not relevance.

**2. The problem gets a face.**
Instead of describing a pain point, they cast it as a character to be defeated: Mama Doom
(inner-critic voice, Learning Dynamics), Dazzeroth the bed demon (Sheets & Giggles),
Pestergeist (Riddy pest control), the evil-baby narrator sabotaging its own socks (Squid
Socks), the lonely-banker supervillain whose defeat *is* the brand story (Honor Credit
Union), the "Low T Force" of low-testosterone superheroes. This does two things: it makes
the pain point entertaining to sit through, and it gives the ad a villain to defeat in Act
3, which doubles as the emotional payoff.

**3. Skeptic + expert = the exposition engine.**
Nearly every single ad, regardless of genre, runs on one core mechanic: character A
doubts/mocks/misunderstands, character B (often literally an employee, spokesperson, or
the product's rep) answers with a spec, character A reacts with a joke, repeat 8–15
times. This is how feature dumps get through without ever feeling like a feature dump —
every spec is the answer to a question a real skeptical customer would ask ("does that
mean I can play the long game on Bitcoin," "is it hard to set up," "wait, is creatine
illegal"). Watch for this — it is the single most repeatable, teachable piece of the
formula and the easiest to brief a writer on.

**4. Stat, then immediately a joke.**
Any time a real number shows up (70% of dogs have anxiety, 50% of VC managers beat the
market, testosterone down 50% since 1975, carpets hold 40 lbs of dust/year), it's followed
within one line by a punchline that defuses it. Numbers never get to sit there and feel
like a stat block.

**5. Physical, visual "burden of proof" demos.**
A recurring device: put the product and the old way side by side on camera. Burn two
towels to show which one's real cotton (Wet Cat). Split a carpet in half, walk on both
(ZeroRes). Two mason jars of hard vs. soft water shaken with soap (Watertech). This is
their stand-in for a claims chart — dramatized, not stated.

**6. Price reframed as an absurd comparison, never a bare number.**
"Less than three Stanley mugs" (Aquavis), "6 months' salary" for a mined diamond vs. lab
grown, "lasts as long as your passport" (Saalt cups), a diamond ring joke about a "50%
chance the marriage works out, 100% chance the ring does." Price is always translated into
something the audience already has a gut feeling about.

**7. Testimonial burst right before the CTA, not spread throughout.**
Almost every ad clusters 3–5 rapid, unpolished-sounding customer quotes at roughly the
75–85% mark — right after the joke engine winds down and right before the URL is repeated.
This is the "credibility beat" that closes the sale after the entertainment has done its
job.

**8. The CTA is said 2–3 times and is bolted to a final callback joke.**
The site is never said once — "go to X.com" gets restated once mid-ad and once at the very
end, then the ad closes on a joke that returns to the cold open's bit (the dwarfs ask Snow
White to "bring back a diamond," the evil baby says "don't be a fool, buy some today," the
detective show closes with "boom goes the dynamite"). The joke, not the CTA, is the actual
last line almost every time — the CTA is stated clearly, but the *exit* is comedic, so the
ad is memorable/shareable rather than feeling like it ends on a sales pitch.

**9. Pun density and rhyme as connective tissue.**
Several open in rhyming couplets (DryTote's "got soggy bins / worried about what the
damper is doing to your camper"). Brand names get worked into puns relentlessly (ZeLo
Clean → "CeeLo clean," Airlift → "stop squatting, start towing," Catalyst Pet → "keep the
cat, ditch the smell"). This is a stylistic tic, not filler — it's doing a lot of the
"this is entertainment, not an ad" work.

## What their self-promotional ads reveal (they say the formula out loud)

Several of the 85 videos are Creatably advertising *itself* (Influencer Mill, More Leads,
Million Dollar Ad, Go Behind the Scenes, We Are Creatably), and in those they state their
philosophy directly rather than hiding it in subtext:

- They explicitly position against influencer/UGC ads: "with influencers your brand is
  just a prop, with Creatably your brand is the star."
- They're allergic to being style-first: "we don't just tell a good story, we put our
  money where our mouth is" — every ad they cite comes with a number attached (10x ROAS,
  $10M in closed revenue, tripled revenue, 30x ROAS).
- Their own tagline, stated flat: "is it awesome yet?" — used as the internal editing
  filter during production.
- They deliberately reuse assets across their own portfolio as running gags — Pillow Cube
  and Thread Wallets both get referenced/cameoed inside *other* clients' ads ("holy
  mackerel, Mr. President, it's a giant pillow cube"). It reads like an intentional shared
  universe across their client roster, which also works as a subtle "look how many brands
  trust us" flex.

## The recipe, distilled

If you wanted to brief a script in Creatably's voice, the checklist is:

1. Pick a genre/format that has nothing to do with the category, purely for the joke.
2. Cold-open inside that genre, no product mentioned for the first 15–20 seconds.
3. Turn the pain point into a character/villain, not a description.
4. Cast a skeptic and an expert; let every spec come out as an answer to a skeptical
   question, never as a list.
5. Every real number gets a joke within one line.
6. Include one physical side-by-side demo if the product has any visual proof at all.
7. Reframe price as a relatable object comparison, never state it bare.
8. Cluster 3–5 real-sounding testimonials right before the close.
9. State the URL twice; end on a callback joke to the cold open, not on the URL.
10. Keep total runtime 2–4.5 minutes — long enough for a real three-act mini-sitcom.
