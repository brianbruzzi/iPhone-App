A field guide built from 85 of their ads

The Creatably Playbook

How one small studio writes comedy commercials that actually sell — broken into rules you can use tonight, whether it's your first script for class or your fortieth for a client.

Plain text is for everyone. Highlighted lines are the parts to remember. Pro Note blocks are the professional layer — the craft detail behind each rule. Script excerpts are lightly cleaned from YouTube's auto-captions of the real ads.

Every ad is a three-act sitcom that happens to sell something.

Before any rule, learn the shape. It's the same in all 85 videos, and it's why they run two to four minutes instead of thirty seconds.

ACT I

Cold open. Start inside a scene, in a genre that has nothing to do with the product. No logo, no "are you tired of…". The product isn't named for the first 15 seconds.

ACT II

The double-act. A Skeptic and an Expert trade questions and answers. Every feature comes out as an answer to a doubtful question — never as a list.

ACT III

Proof. A fast burst of real customers, then the price reframed as something you've already bought.

EXIT

Say the website. End on the joke. The URL gets said twice; the last line is a callback to the opening bit.

Pro Note

Two to four-and-a-half minutes isn't self-indulgence — it's the minimum for three acts. A :30 can't hold this structure. Creatably shoots the long version as the master and cuts the short ones down from it, so the cutdowns inherit the jokes and the proof.

The nine rules

Ordered by how often they showed up across the 85 ads. Rule 3 is the one that shows up in almost every single one.

Rule 1 · Genre

Put the whole ad in a costume.Pick a movie genre or TV format for its jokes, not for whether it matches the product.

A heist. A police stop. A game show. A fairy tale. A medieval court. The ad borrows a format everyone already knows, so it doesn't feel like an ad while it's teaching you the pitch. The genre is never chosen for relevance. A mattress got the French Revolution. A credit union got a supervillain's lonely monologue.

Bacon (temp staffing) → spy movie  ·  Luminous (inventory software) → Lord of the Rings  ·  Kizik (shoes) → heist  ·  Lab Grown (diamonds) → game show  ·  iSleep (sleep apnea) → Snow White  ·  US Virtual Notary → medieval kingdom

From: Cosset — "Speed" (bath bombs)
Bus driverEverybody remain calm. There are bombs on this bus.
PassengerNot again.
HeroDon't panic. You just need to keep this bus going twenty miles per hour. No more, no less.
PassengerThat's so slow.
Try it

Write your product on a card. Pull a genre out of a hat — heist, courtroom, nature documentary, cooking show, horror. Pitch the first fifteen seconds out loud before you're allowed to say the product's name.

Pro Note

A genre earns its place if you can list six of its tropes in under a minute — each trope is a free bit. Genre also pre-loads production design (wardrobe, sets, music cues), which is why these spots look more expensive than they are.

Rule 2 · The villain

Give the problem a face.Don't describe the pain point. Cast it.

Learning Dynamics turned a parent's anxiety into Mama Doom, an inner demon narrating worst-case scenarios. Sheets & Giggles made overheating a demon under the bed. Squid Socks let the baby narrate his own sock-removal schemes like a Bond villain. When the problem is a character, it can be beaten — and the product beating it is your ending.

From: Learning Dynamics — "Mama Doom" (reading program)
MomWe've been here twenty minutes. It's Hop on Pop, not Shakespeare.
Mama DoomShe probably won't get a job. Then wind up in jail.
MomMaybe Lucy just won't be much of a reader.
Mama DoomOh sure. She can just watch soaps all day.
Try it

Name your villain. What does it whisper to the customer at 2 a.m.? Write the one line it says that gets a laugh because it's a little bit true.

Pro Note

The villain has to be something the product genuinely defeats in Act III, or the resolution reads as fake. Write the defeat first, then design the villain backward from it.

Rule 3 · The engine

Skeptic + Expert.The one mechanic that runs nearly every ad, regardless of genre.

Two characters. The Skeptic doubts, mocks, or doesn't get it. The Expert answers with a real fact about the product. The Skeptic reacts — and the reaction is the joke. Repeat eight to fifteen times. This is how an ad gets through a dozen features without ever sounding like a feature list: every spec is the answer to a question a real, doubtful customer would ask.

From: Aquavis — "Water Waiter" (under-sink filter)
CustomerNever mind. I'll just get my pitcher filter.
WaiterWhen was the last time you changed that filter? Madam, you have to change those. Pitcher filters are only rated for forty to a hundred gallons. The AquaVis filter delivers five hundred gallons between changes.
CustomerThis pitcher only lasts, like, a day. Or one Stanley mug, if I have to refill it.
Try it

List the five questions a doubtful friend would ask about your product — those are the Skeptic's lines. Answer each in one true sentence — those are the Expert's. Now give the Skeptic a reaction to every answer. You just wrote Act II.

Pro Note

The Skeptic's questions are your objection list from sales calls — write those before a single joke. The Expert almost never gets the punchline; the laughs live in the Skeptic's reactions. If a feature can't be phrased as the answer to a question, it doesn't go in the ad.

Rule 4 · Numbers

A stat, then a joke.Within one line. Every time.

Real statistics show up constantly — testosterone down 50% since 1975, carpets absorb forty pounds of dirt a year, a billion people have sleep apnea. And every number is followed within one line by a punchline that lets the audience off the hook. The number never gets to sit there and feel like a slide.

From: Tearribles — "Crime Scene" (dog toys)
NarratorA study from Texas A&M found that nearly fifty percent of dogs suffer from anxiety, and almost ninety percent have some kind of behavioral issue. And you do not want to go to dog therapy.
Dog (on the couch)They always blame the dog, Mom.
Try it

Find one real number about the problem your product solves. Write three jokes about the number. Keep the shortest one.

Pro Note

The joke should be about the stat, not a non-sequitur after it — the laugh is what makes the number stick. Put the source on screen; it costs nothing, and the joke has already bought you the trust to be believed.

Rule 5 · Proof

Prove it on camera.Where another ad would show a chart, they run a test.

Wet Cat burns two towels to show which one is real cotton. ZeroRes cleans half a carpet and lets a food court walk on both halves. Watertech shakes two jars of water with soap. Product and old way, side by side, in the same frame. It's a comparison chart you can't look away from.

From: Wet Cat — "Foundational" (Turkish towels)
WifeThe other Turkish towels claim they're one hundred percent cotton. But fire doesn't lie.
She lights the competitor's towel. It melts.
WifeBlack smoke. That means it's blended with polyester — a.k.a. plastic.
HusbandSo the people on the internet lied? Is nothing sacred anymore?
Try it

What's the simplest test someone could do at their kitchen table that shows your product winning? Describe it in one sentence. If you can't, find a different claim.

Pro Note

One variable. Both sides visible in the same shot. Narrated by the Skeptic's reaction, not the Expert's claim — the audience trusts a bystander's "wait, what?" more than a spokesperson's "as you can see."

Rule 6 · Price

Price is a comparison, never a number."Less than three Stanley mugs."

A mined diamond costs "six months' salary." A period cup "lasts as long as your passport." The price is always translated into something the audience has already bought without guilt — so the purchase feels like something they already do, not a new decision.

From: Aquavis — "Water Waiter"
CustomerI don't know if I can afford it.
WaiterYou can get AquaVis for less than three Stanley mugs.
CustomerI've definitely bought more than three Stanley mugs.
WaiterYes. I am aware.
Try it

Finish the sentence: "It costs less than ______." Aim for something your audience is slightly embarrassed to have spent money on.

Pro Note

Anchor to an item from the same wallet: an impulse buy for impulse products, a monthly bill for subscriptions, a lifetime total for anything with a ten-year lifespan. The comparison should also do the math for them.

Rule 7 · Testimonials

Bunch the real people at the end.Three to five quick clips, right before the website.

Real customers don't get sprinkled through the ad. They arrive as one fast burst at roughly the 75–85% mark — just after the jokes wind down, just before the URL gets repeated. It's the moment the ad switches from "entertained" to "convinced."

Try it

Watch any one of these ads and write down the timestamp when the first real customer appears. Then check it against the runtime.

Pro Note

Rough audio and unrehearsed phrasing read as real — don't over-polish this beat. Cut fast, three to five voices, no names on screen unless they add credibility. This is the beat that closes the sale the comedy opened.

Rule 8 · The exit

Say the website twice. End on the joke.The last line is almost never the URL.

The site name gets said clearly at least twice — once mid-ad, once at the close. But the actual last line is a callback to the cold open, not the call to action. It's what makes the ad feel like a short film instead of a commercial, and it's why people send it to a friend.

From: iSleep — "Snow White" (sleep apnea testing)
Snow WhiteVisit iSleep.com, where a better night's sleep is no fairy tale.
The seven dwarfs march off to the mine.
Snow WhiteBring me back a diamond, boys.
Try it

Write your last line as a joke that only makes sense if you watched the first ten seconds.

Pro Note

The callback makes it rewatchable and shareable; the URL makes it accountable. Both, in that order. Say the URL at conversational speed and spell out anything ambiguous — auto-captions garbled half the site names in these ads, which means viewers do too.

Rule 9 · Wordplay

Puns are structure, not filler."Stop squatting. Start towing."

Brand names get worked into wordplay relentlessly. Some ads open in rhyme (DryTote: "got soggy bins? worried what the damper is doing to your camper?"). Catalyst Pet closes on "keep the cat, ditch the smell." The puns are doing a job: telling the audience "this is entertainment, relax."

Try it

Write five puns on your brand name. Four will be bad. Keep the fifth for the tagline.

Pro Note

Pun density is highest in the cold open and the exit — the two places the viewer decides whether to keep watching and whether to click. Act II stays cleaner so the facts land.

They say the formula out loud.

Six of the 85 videos are Creatably advertising itself. In those, the philosophy isn't hidden in subtext.

"With influencers, your brand is just a prop. With Creatably, your brand is the star."Creatably — "Influencer Mill"
"We don't just tell a good story. We put our money where our mouth is."Creatably — "We are Creatably"
"Is it awesome yet? … How about now?"Creatably — "Million Dollar Ad" (their editing question)

Every campaign they cite comes with a number attached — a 10× or 30× ROAS, revenue tripled, "$10 million in closed revenue." (ROAS is return on ad spend: dollars earned per dollar of ads. If that term is new to you, it's the only piece of jargon in this guide.)

Pro Note

They cameo their own past clients inside new clients' ads — Pillow Cube turns up in the Creatably reel and in a mattress spot, Thread Wallets in two others. It reads as a running gag and works as a portfolio flex. If you build a body of work, let it reference itself.
